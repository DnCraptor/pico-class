**Curriculum — Part 1 "General Concept"**  
**Revision:** 0.4 (30 September 2026)  
**Status:** working draft; teaching modules 0–34 have been written (list in §1.6), their distribution over school years and hours has not been approved yet.

## 1.1. Title and focus

**"Computer Class: Fundamentals of Information Technology"**.

Focus: educational and technical, with elements of history and organised leisure.

The programme gives children a first introduction to information technology, to how computers work, to the history of computing and to the basics of programming.

The programme is not tied to a particular form of organisation: it can be used for self-study, school after-class activities and classes in out-of-school (supplementary) education centres.

The programme does not claim to be complete or to work under every circumstance; it only lets you build your own plan on top of it and save some time.

## 1.2. Main idea

To create an affordable learning environment in which children get to know information technology by working directly with computers and software.

The distinctive feature of the class is the use of lightweight emulators and software that do not need modern high-performance hardware.

The history of personal computers is used as one way of introducing information technology step by step: from relatively simple computer systems and programs to more complex ones.

The emphasis is on hands-on learning, independent experiments and growing interest in how computers work and what they can do.

Alongside the lessons there is free time for computer games, mostly classic ones that do not need powerful hardware.

## 1.3. Main areas

### Computer literacy

- The main parts of a computer and what they are for.
- Input, output and storage devices.
- Operating systems and application programs.
- Files, directories, file systems.
- Basic ways of working with a computer.

### History of computing

The main stages in the development of personal computers and software, historical platforms, their technical capabilities, interfaces and programs. Demonstrations mostly use software emulators running on affordable hardware.

### Programming basics

- Algorithms and sequences of commands.
- Variables and simple operations on data.
- Conditions and loops.
- Input and output.
- Writing, running and changing small programs.
- Finding and fixing simple errors.

The specific languages and environments are chosen when the lesson plan is drawn up, taking into account the pupils' age and the available software set.

### Practical work

Independent tasks, experiments with programs and operating systems, small projects and exploring what the computer can do.

### Computer games and leisure

Classic games as part of the history of software and interactive entertainment. There is free play time, not necessarily linked to the lessons. Games can also illustrate how graphics, sound and interfaces developed.

## 1.4. Principles

**Low cost.** Preference goes to affordable hardware, free software, open-source software and software licensed for free educational use.

**Modest hardware requirements.** The programme relies on tools that run on low-power hardware and does not require high-performance PCs.

**Practical focus.** Theory comes with demonstrations, exercises and independent work.

**Accessible to beginners.** No previous programming knowledge or special technical skills are needed.

**Step-by-step learning.** The material becomes harder gradually — from simple ideas and actions to algorithms, systems and independent projects.

**Flexibility.** The pace and content of the lessons can be adapted to the pupils' age, background and interests. The core modules run on the minimal hardware configuration.

**Independence and exploration.** Experiments and finding solutions on one's own are encouraged.

**Learning and leisure together.** Free play and exploring programs on one's own have value in themselves and do not need to be justified by a lesson.

## 1.5. Expected outcomes

By the end of the introductory course the pupils should have a first understanding of:

- The main parts of a computer and how they work together.
- What operating systems and application software are for.
- Files, directories and how information is stored.
- The main stages in the development of personal computers.
- How algorithms and programs are built.
- Software emulation of computer systems.

Expected practical skills:

- Starting and using the available programs.
- Basic operations with files and directories.
- Working with simple operating-system interfaces.
- Writing simple algorithms.
- Writing and running very simple programs.
- Finding and fixing elementary errors.
- Doing small practical tasks on one's own.

An additional goal is to develop interest in information technology and in technical creativity.

**Firmware names in the teaching modules** are written with the tag `<BOARD>` in place of the board code: `<BOARD>-bk-…` means `PCp2-bk-…` on the Olimex RP2040-PICO-PC + Pico 2, `z0p2-bk-…` on the Waveshare RP2350-PiZero and `m2p2-bk-…` on the Murmulator 2.0 (the board codes are listed at the top of [`hardware/COMPATIBILITY.md`](hardware/COMPATIBILITY.md)). The ellipsis stands for the rest of the name: video output, sound, clock and version differ between the builds for different boards. If several variants of one firmware are built for a board (for example, for the Murmulator 2.0 — VGA or HDMI, PWM or I2S), the board's instructions say which one to use.

**If there is no firmware for your board**, or it does not work: every firmware has a repository (links are in the board's `MANIFEST.md` and in [`hardware/COMPATIBILITY.md`](hardware/COMPATIBILITY.md)). First look at its **Releases** section — there are often ready-made builds for different boards, sometimes newer than the ones in the kit. If there is nothing there, or the build does not work, write to the author in the **Issues** section of the same repository or ask in the Telegram support groups (in Russian): [t.me/ZX_MURMULATOR](https://t.me/ZX_MURMULATOR) and [t.me/murmulator_talks](https://t.me/murmulator_talks). Test any firmware that does not come from the kit on one workstation before the lesson (section 5 of the board's instructions). Which firmwares are built and accepted on each board is listed in [`hardware/COMPATIBILITY.md`](hardware/COMPATIBILITY.md); if a module's firmware is missing for a board, the module is not taught on that board — there is no equipment for it.

**Younger pupils in programming lessons.** Programming languages require reading fluently, typing Latin letters confidently and keeping several steps in mind at once; this usually comes at the age of 8–9, but the teacher decides from observation (teaching module 0, "Assessing the level"), not from age. Pupils who are not ready yet listen to the talk and watch the demonstration with everyone else, and during practical time follow their own track — also about algorithms, but without text: games with "executors" without a computer, the PRIMARY page in LogoWriter (teaching module 18), the TypeFast typing tutor; the order of work is described in the optional teaching module 33. Ordinary games stay in the free play time, as in the other modules. These pupils sit apart from those who write programs, sound only through headphones; once per lesson an older pupil comes over to help the younger ones. The "Adaptation" part of each module in this area says what exactly the younger pupils do in that lesson.

The balance between the forms of activity is set by the lesson plan. Games are organised with regard to the pupils' age, the content of the games and the class rules.

## 1.7. Equipment

The computer class is built on affordable microcontroller platforms with the **RP2350** and a set of specialised firmwares. The main criteria are low cost, a reproducible set of equipment, availability of new parts, easy preparation and maintenance, and a wide range of learning and leisure uses.

The core modules run on the minimal configuration (§1.7.3). Recommended equipment (§1.7.6) makes some modules more convenient, but the module can be done without it. Additional equipment (§1.7.6) extends the possibilities and is needed only for optional modules.

### 1.7.1. Recommended hardware platform

**Main recommendation:** **Olimex RP2040-PICO-PC + a compatible Raspberry Pi Pico 2 (RP2350) board**.

Reasons:

- Mass-produced parts instead of depending on random batches of written-off equipment.
- No soldering of small chips is needed to build a basic workstation.
- It has an audio output jack, so no separate DAC has to be added.
- It uses an SD card and replaceable firmwares.
- The Pico 2 microcontroller board can be replaced separately from the Olimex main board.

Sound support must be built into a particular firmware build. Having a jack does not by itself guarantee that sound works in an arbitrary firmware.

### 1.7.2. Alternative hardware platforms

**Waveshare RP2350-PiZero** is an alternative. When choosing it, keep in mind the reported build-quality issues of some boards and the lack of a ready-made analogue audio output. Sound needs an extra solution: PWM audio or an external DAC of the PCM510x family. The extra circuitry adds to the cost and effort of equipping the class. For a class on this board, sound is fixed as an external I2S module with a PCM5102A DAC; workstation instructions: [`hardware/waveshare-rp2350-pizero/README.en.md`](hardware/waveshare-rp2350-pizero/README.en.md).

**Murmulator 1.x / 2.0-compatible boards with the RP2350** are further options with open design files and community support. You can build them yourself or buy them from independent makers. Without standardised production control, the quality of assembly and parts can vary; this is an extra risk when equipping a whole class. The **Murmulator 2.0** has its own audio output (TDA1387 DAC or PWM), HDMI or VGA and a socket for a replaceable Pico 2 module, so the workstation needs no additional modules; instructions: [`hardware/murmulator20/README.en.md`](hardware/murmulator20/README.en.md). FRANK boards (rh1tech) are pin-compatible with the Murmulator 2 as well; they have not been tested in the class.

### 1.7.3. Minimum system requirements

| Component | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Computing platform | RP2350 on a board supported by the chosen firmware manager |
| RAM | Built-in SRAM of the RP2350 |
| Video output | VGA or HDMI (depending on the supported board and firmware) |
| Monitor | Compatible with the chosen video output |
| TV composite | Optional, including for a demonstration station with a CRT TV |
| Keyboard | PS/2 or USB HID; at least 101 keys for full compatibility |
| Firmware and data storage | SD card, FAT32 with 4 KB clusters, SPI interface |
| Audio output | **Required** |
| Mouse | USB HID; recommended (§1.7.6) |
| Game controller | NES DPAD, optional |

Keyboards without a NumPad are allowed, with the caveat that programs using the full set of PC keyboard keys will not be fully usable.

The table describes **the allowed options across the family of supported configurations**, not the presence of all listed interfaces on every board. In particular, the type of video output and the keyboard interface depend on the specific board and firmware build.

### 1.7.4. Audio equipment

Sound is a **required** part of the equipment, because it is needed for games, for demonstrating multimedia capabilities and for learning the history of computer sound.

| Station | Audio equipment |
|---|---|
| Pupil's station | Headphones |
| Teacher's station | Powered speakers |
| Demonstration station | Powered speakers |

Volume control is needed. Individual headphones reduce interference between stations.

Some firmwares output sound not only to the board's audio output but also over HDMI. So that running such a firmware on several stations at once does not turn into a cacophony, **the volume of the monitors' built-in speakers is set to zero** when the class is prepared, and checked before each lesson. Pupils hear sound only through headphones.

### 1.7.5. Software platform and firmware management

The learning environment is built **not around a single firmware** but around a set of firmwares for different learning and leisure tasks. A boot manager switches between them.

The open-source projects used:

- **pico-launcher** — the main manager for the beginner level: file browser, launching firmwares, viewing and simple editing of text files: https://github.com/DnCraptor/pico-launcher
- **murmulator-os2** — an advanced environment with a command line, a file manager and BASIC: https://github.com/DnCraptor/murmulator-os2/releases

When choosing programs, preference goes to firmwares that support several hardware configurations, video modes and peripheral options. A single firmware does not have to support all options at once.

The results of testing firmwares on the supported boards are kept in the compatibility matrix: [`hardware/COMPATIBILITY.md`](hardware/COMPATIBILITY.md) (in Russian). The SD-card content common to all boards — programs, games, FreeDOS, MOS2 — is in [`sdcard/`](sdcard/) and described in [`SDCARD.en.md`](SDCARD.en.md); files built for a particular board are in `hardware/<board>/sdcard/`.

The source code allows the software to be adapted to compatible hardware. However, the standard preparation of a class must not require changes to the source code or modifications to the boards.

### 1.7.6. Recommended and additional equipment

**Recommended equipment** is not required: the modules work without it, but it makes work easier and faster.

- **A USB HID mouse and a USB hub with an OTG adapter.** Needed in teaching module 17 (graphical interface, Macintosh emulator). The PCp2 has a single USB port: through a hub it takes both a USB keyboard and a mouse; with a PS/2 keyboard the mouse is plugged into USB directly, without a hub. Without a mouse the pointer is moved with the keyboard's numeric keypad (NumPad) or an NES DPAD gamepad — slower, but the module can still be done. Before buying for the whole class, test the chosen mouse and hub on one workstation.

**Additional equipment**, depending on the module:

- External PSRAM. It is not required: the modules that need extra memory are among the optional modules.
- NES DPAD controllers.
- An ESP32 board (ESP32-WROOM-32 or ESP32-WROVER module) with a CH340 USB-UART and a USB hub — a Wi-Fi modem on COM1 of the murm386 emulator (PSRAM build) for teaching module 35; a Wi-Fi network with Internet access is needed too, preferably a separate guest network.
- A separate demonstration station with composite video output and a CRT TV.
- A projector with an HDMI input instead of the monitor at the teacher's station. It lets you show demonstrations and go through tasks with the whole group at once; for small groups that can all see the teacher's screen it is not needed. The projector must support the video modes of the firmwares used (at least 640×480 at 60 Hz, and 720×576 at 50 Hz for PICO-BK); sound for the audience still goes to the powered speakers.
- Additional electronic components, interface boards and sensors for experiments.

Modules that need additional equipment are optional. Modules can also be optional **by age**: they are needed only if the class has the corresponding group of pupils (teaching module 33 is for the youngest, roughly 6–9 years old, who are not ready for programming languages yet). The class leader can make such a module part of the required programme if all workstations have the equipment.

### 1.7.7. Reproducibility and maintenance

New mass-produced boards and parts that can be bought again and replaced are preferred for equipping the class. The workstations are made as uniform as possible to simplify preparing SD cards, installing firmwares, everyday use and repair.

**Main configuration:** Olimex RP2040-PICO-PC + Pico 2, a compatible HDMI monitor, a keyboard, a FAT32 SD card and headphones. The teacher's and demonstration stations get powered speakers instead of headphones.

Alternative platforms are acceptable if they provide what the required lessons need. This matters especially in regions where delivery of parts from Europe is limited; there it is recommended to rely on compatible platforms that can be ordered from China.

When choosing a board, prefer the largest flash size and, if available, PSRAM. With a small flash chip some games will not load, because they can be larger than the available flash. PSRAM greatly speeds up the firmwares that can use it.

### 1.7.8. Handling the boards with care

All supported boards — Olimex, RP2350-PiZero, Murmulator — work **without a case**: contacts and traces are exposed on both sides. So in the first lesson (teaching module 0, class rules), and later whenever needed, pupils are reminded:

- no drinks near the workstation: tea spilled on a running board is a short circuit;
- do not put paper clips, coins, keys, screwdrivers or other metal or conductive objects next to or on the board;
- do not slide the board across the desk on its underside: the contacts on the back scratch the desk and get scratched themselves, and small parts can break off;
- the board is moved and turned over only with the power off, holding it by the edges.

A **soft mat** goes under each board — a mouse pad at least. It protects the contacts on the back and the desk, and above all, when the RESET and BOOTSEL buttons are pressed, the board does not press its pins against a hard surface. The mat is part of the workstation kit.
